Stage 5 already questions laws and changes them democratically, yet slide 22 asks *Why do we need another level beyond this?* The answer is also the reason conventional morality is not where moral reasoning should stop.

## Conventional morality is only as good as the conventions it copies

The course notes place the conventional level between the self-centred and the self-consciously principled levels: it is where *human beings have been socialized into accepting the customary moral attitudes and judgements manifested in their families, neighbourhoods, the corporate world, and the broader society*. Then the key sentence: *To the extent that any of these attitudes and judgements are either flawed or may be only accidentally right, conventional morality tends to be a very poor guide to ethical conduct.*

*Flawed* is the obvious failure: the convention is wrong and you copy it. *Only accidentally right* is the subtle one: the convention happens to be right, so you act rightly, but your reason (it is what we do here) would have led you just as readily to a wrong act. Post-conventional reasoning is *more self-consciously critical of existing moral norms and their typical scope of application*, and in the notes' words it *has helped undermine a range of discriminatory attitudes toward women, racial minorities, gays and lesbians, and many others as well*.

Democracy can fail, and slide 23 is stage 6, *universal ethical principles (beyond democracy, civil disobedience, creating/rewriting constitution)*:

- *Few reach this stage. The "prophet" stage.*
- *Lists of moral rules may be abandoned in favour of deeper wisdom. Example: realization that even democratic processes may fail (e.g. Trump, Brexit, rising popularity of far right leaders) – suppose that a majority decides to elect someone who hurts a minority, the so-called "tyranny of the majority" (John Stuart Mill, Thomas Jefferson)*
- *The Aristotelian question is: does the majority rule for its own advantage, or for the common advantage?*
- *One distinguishing issue - Possibility of justification of civil disobedience. If a law cannot be changed by democratic processes, disobedience can be morally justified.*
- *Martin Luther King, for example, argued that laws are only valid insofar as they are grounded in justice, and that a commitment to justice carries with it an obligation to disobey unjust laws. (Kohlberg, 1981, p. 43).*

Stage 5 asks whether a law was made fairly; stage 6 asks whether it is just.

## Singer is the lecture's worked rejection of convention

Slide 24, *Peter Singer – "all animals are equal": Challenging convention/tradition*. Singer is a *Utilitarian* who *argued for equal rights for animals based on "equal consideration of interests"* ([lecture 4's version](#/libs7102-l4/equal-consideration-of-interests)), but *what is relevant for this course is his rejection of convention*. The slide's computing example: *Conventions in computing in the 90s - hacking is exciting/getting pirated software is cheaper/getting copyrighted movies is smart/"it's not illegal if you don't get caught". Has anything changed since then?* The last is stage 1 reasoning held by a whole group. *Recently Singer has argued that machines may one day also deserve equal rights, if conscious*. *Do you agree?*

His argument, taught in full in [the next lesson](#/libs7102-l2/singer-all-animals-are-equal), runs in four steps (slides 25 to 31): equality is a moral ideal, not a fact; it means equal consideration of interests, not the same rights; it rests on the capacity to suffer, not merit; and preferring our own species without a further reason is speciesism. The notes say the point of the reading *is not so much to convert you* but *to reveal the difference between moral convictions which rest on principled reasoning and those which rest more on the imperatives of cultural conformity and the forward momentum of tradition. Some of that tradition will easily withstand critical scrutiny, some of it will not.*
