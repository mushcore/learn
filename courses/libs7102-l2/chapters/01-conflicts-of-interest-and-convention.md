A conflict of interest asks you to choose between your employer and yourself, and the quickest way out is to do what everyone around you does. Lecture 2 asks whether that way out is good enough.

## A conflict of interest sets loyalty to the company against your own interest

Slide 3 is titled *Examples of conflicts of interest – be loyal to the company or serve your own interest?* and lists ten:

1. *Offering paid services on the side to a client of your company*
2. *Accepting a favour from another company for information about your employer*
3. *Failing to investigate a subordinate's wrongdoing because they are a friend*
4. *Working for your company, you purchase software to boost another business that you have a stake in*
5. *Accepting consulting fees and providing advice to a competitor of your company*
6. *Sharing information in a job interview about your current employer's market strategy*
7. *Taking advantage of confidential information learned on the job for your own benefit*
8. *Taking advantage of a business opportunity learn while at work that your company may have pursued*
9. *Starting a competitor company that provides services similar to your employer*
10. *Making arrangements to work for a client at a future date while your company continues to do business with them*

The notes' verdict is about method: *One way to deal with these conflicts of interest is to follow convention, tradition, or habit. That is, simply do what is usually done in your field, or perhaps in your company or among your peers. Such conventional morality, however, often gets it wrong.* Kohlberg and Singer, the lecture's two authors, *both challenged the assumption that morality is just a set of rules that gets passed on*. Rejecting convention is not rejecting loyalty: the notes add that post-conventional reasoning helps us see *why approaches to solving conflicts of interest may be wrong, and why loyalty (e.g. to your company) has value*.

## Convention answers the dilemma by asking what others do

Slide 4, *Ethics and convention*, asks *What is best to solve the moral dilemma?*

1. *Ask your co-workers what they usually do*
2. *Figure out what your boss does. It should be OK to do the same*
3. *What is more likely to get you in trouble, considering past situations in the company (who got in trouble for what)*
4. *?*

All three answers find the right act in what the people around you do or punish, never in whether the act itself is right. The fourth line is blank on purpose: the rest of the lecture fills it.

## The week's question is whether existing social norms define what is moral

Slide 5 opens with *Now we know there is genuine moral action. But how do we know how to act morally?*, the result of lecture 1 (psychological egoism is false; slides 8 to 11 of this deck review it, and slide 12 reviews the validity trick questions). Then: *Convention? Do existing social norms define what's moral? Is learning ethics the memorization of current rules?* *Maybe not - Kohlberg's levels of moral development (suggests moral knowledge requires cognitive development, not complying with convention)*. And: *All animals are equal (and machines will have rights, too!) – Singer rejects convention*.

## In philosophy you cannot look a definition up

Slide 7 makes two points. First, *Googling/using AI for definitions often fails*: *In the sciences, terms often have a single well-defined definition (e.g. sequoia - Sequoiadendron giganteum, or prime number)*, but that is *not the case in philosophy*. *Google "objectivism" and you'll likely get Ayn Rand*, which is not the course's sense (lecture 3, slide 28 gives Pojman's). Second, *using the dictionary* is *typically not a good starting point to solve philosophical problems*: *Look up "happiness" in the dictionary to write an essay on happiness* and you learn only usage, because *the dictionary isn't meant to solve philosophical problems but just tell us about common usage of the word*. On the exam, define a term as the lecture defines it and say whose definition it is.
