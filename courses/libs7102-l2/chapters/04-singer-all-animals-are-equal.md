title: "Singer: equality means equal consideration of interests, and the test is suffering"
minutes: 25

Singer argues that the reason for human equality reaches beyond humans: reasoning against a convention.

## Arguing for a group with no recognized rights sounds ridiculous, as it once did for women

Slide 25: *It can sound ridiculous to argue for the rights of a group that, by convention, has no (recognized) rights.* When Mary Wollstonecraft argued for the rights of women, Thomas Taylor joked: *"Then why don't we also argue for the rights of brutes!"* Singer takes the joke seriously: *Equality extends to both women AND animals because in both cases empirical fact is not the basis for equality*, and the slide adds *note his "no ought from is" argument*: how individuals differ is a fact, and facts alone give no conclusion about rights.

## Equal rights are not the same rights

Slide 26: men and women are not *the same in every physical respect*, so *we don't need to have the same rights, just equal rights* (*men cannot have children*, so no one defends their right to an abortion). *Pigs cannot vote, so it's meaningless to speak of their right to vote.* Singer's conclusion: *a.* there is *an obvious difference between animals and humans*; *b. It doesn't follow from a. that we should reject equality in either case!* So *what should it be based on?*

## Equality is a moral ideal: equal consideration of interests

Slide 27: *"Equality is a moral ideal, not a statement of a fact"* (*we are obviously not the same!*). *The ideal is impartiality (utilitarianism's "equal consideration of interests")*, in Sidgwick's words: *"The good of any one individual is of no more importance, from the point of view (if I may say so) of the Universe, than the good of any other."* The slide asks *how can we justify it?* Lecture 4 makes this principle the heart of utilitarianism ([equal consideration of interests](#/libs7102-l4/equal-consideration-of-interests)).

## The basis is not merit but the capacity to suffer

Slide 28: *Equality should not be grounded on any kind of merit.* *Possessing a higher degree of intelligence does not entitle one human to use another for his own ends. It should also not entitle humans to exploit nonhumans.* Singer quotes Bentham: the question is not *can they talk or reason*, but *"Can they suffer?"*, *the vital characteristic that gives a being the right to equal consideration*.

## Slide 29 tests our treatment of animals with an experimenter's choice

Slide 29 starts from how *we rear them, we eat them, we experiment on them*, and then gives a case.

Slide 29's verdict is speciesism, a bias toward one's own species: *the experimenter shows a bias towards his own species*. Its lesson is the course's: *we should question the assumptions of our age (i.e. we mustn't allow mere convention to dictate our ethics)*. Its photos of robots tie it to slide 24's machine rights.

## Frankena says humans are special; Singer says every being can have a good life

Slide 30: Frankena holds that *basing social justice on merit leads to inegalitarian results*, so humans are equals *not because they are equal, in any respect, but because they are human*: they have emotions, desires and thought, *and hence are capable of enjoying a good life in a sense in which other animals are not*. The slide calls this *justifying an anthropocentric morality*: *humans are special and uniquely deserving of rights!*

Slide 31, Singer's reply: *Frankena's wrong! Every being is capable of having a good life*. We cannot say *all and only humans have some intrinsic dignity*: some humans with severe cognitive impairments *are below the intelligence, sentience, etc. of many non human animals*, yet *do have the same rights*. So rights do not follow Frankena's capacities. *We simply prefer animals of our species. Most of us are speciesists.*

## The discussion questions turn the argument on computing

Slide 32 asks whether we are speciesists *in the empirical sense* and *in the moral sense* (*akin to racism*); *What are the most problematic moral conventions in computing today?*; and, given a *"duty of loyalty"*, whether *what should be done can't just depend on what is usually done*. It ends: *aren't we all speciesists?* (*rat vs person*).
