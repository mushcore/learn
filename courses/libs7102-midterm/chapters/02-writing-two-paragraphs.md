Two paragraphs are marked on what they state and argue, not on length. Here are the instructor's criteria, the mistakes lecture 5 lists, and one listed question answered three ways.

## Paragraph one answers the question and defines its terms in the course's words

Open with your position ("No, utilitarianism does not…"). Then give the definitions the question turns on in the slide's words, with the source named ("Pojman defines moral absolutism as…"), and say in your own words what they mean. The course's terms are not the web's: lecture 3's objectivism is not Ayn Rand's.

## Paragraph two gives the example and the argument, and takes a side

Questions *may require that you don't just explain something, but also argue for a particular point (e.g. was X right about Y, and why? - provide an example to support your claim)*.

- **Use the case you are given**, by name; another example answers another question.
- **Make your own example concrete and about computing:** who does what to whom, and what actually happens.
- **Raise the objection, then answer or concede it.** Lecture 5 slide 5: *avoiding mistakes by only dealing with the basics guarantees a low grade*.
- **End on a verdict that follows from the argument:** "I think the reply fails, because…".

## Lecture 5 lists the mistakes that cost marks

Slides 5 to 10 are the instructor's revisions. Do not:

- leave out objections;
- summarize utilitarianism in one sentence: it *cannot be summarized in a single sentence* (slide 8);
- quote Bentham or Mill without *an explanation in your own words*, or calculate utility in place of discussing the theory;
- say it maximizes utility for the majority, which is wrong: *What matters is only total aggregated utility* (slide 6);
- say an act is moral because its benefits outweigh its costs, which is not the procedure (slide 7);
- say we must always respect consent because doing so maximizes utility: that is rule utilitarianism, not act (slide 9);
- say an action is right because it causes happiness, which is wrong: the right action *may cause pain* (slide 10).

From lectures 1 and 3: never call an argument true or a premise valid, and never call "it depends" relativism.

## One listed question, three answers

**U7. What is the "Too high for humanity" objection to utilitarianism, and how has Mill responded to it?** Write yours on paper first, then compare. It is taught in [Mill's three objections](#/libs7102-l5/mills-three-objections).

### A 0-mark answer names the wrong objection

> The too high for humanity objection is that utilitarianism is too demanding, because it says we must give away almost everything we own to strangers in need. Utilitarianism is about aiming for the general good. Mill said *actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness*, so the objection is wrong.

The marker sees Singer's demandingness problem (slides 36 to 38) in place of an objection about motives, a one-sentence summary and an unexplained quote: nothing to tick.

### A passing answer states the objection and the reply but argues nothing

> The objection (slide 14) is that it demands too much to require that people always act from the motive of promoting the general interest of society: when we are kind to a neighbour, we are not thinking of society. Mill replies (slide 15) that this confuses *the rule of action with the motive of it*: ethics tells us our duties, not that duty must be our only motive. So the kind neighbour acts rightly, because rightness depends on actual consequences.

The marker ticks four of six points, the pass line, but finds no test of the reply and no verdict.

### A full answer tests the reply with a case and takes a side

> The objection, in Mill's own description on slide 14, is that *it is exacting (demanding) too much to require that people shall always act from the inducement of promoting the general interests of society*. The slide's example: when we are kind to a neighbour, we are not really moved by the general interest of society, so a theory that asks for that motive asks too much of human beings. Mill replies (slide 15) that the objection confuses *the rule of action with the motive of it*: *It is the business of ethics to tell us what are our duties, or by what test we may know them, but no system of ethics requires that the sole motive of all we do shall be a feeling of duty*. The test is the actual consequences, so the kind neighbour acts rightly whatever moved them.
>
> The reply works against the objection as stated, but only by making motives irrelevant to right and wrong, and slide 15 asks whether that is true. Sandel's doctors (slide 16) test it: judged by actual outcomes, the doctor who meant to exterminate the villagers but left them healthier acted rightly, and the one who meant to heal them but unknowingly infected them acted wrongly, which most of us reject. So would a developer whose trap-the-user dark pattern happened to leave users better off. So Mill avoids demanding saintly motives at the price of a verdict on motives that is hard to accept.

The marker ticks all six: objection and reply in Mill's words, what the reply means, Sandel's doctors and a computing case against it, and a verdict that follows.
