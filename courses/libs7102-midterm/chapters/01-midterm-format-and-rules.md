The midterm asks you to write, not to recognize. This lesson sets out the rules in the instructor's words, what each lecture feeds, and how to spend the two hours.

## The midterm is four or five two-paragraph answers, closed book, on paper

The midterm note gives the whole format: *Midterm exam (20%) to take in class*. *4-5 2-paragraph answers (you will be able to opt out of 1 or more questions - example: answer 4 of 6 options to get your full mark), closed book, 2 hours, on paper.* It sits on **Wednesday Oct 28**, in the lecture slot.

The scope is *lectures 1-5 (up to and including Utilitarianism I and II)*. *The questions will either be taken from the list of final exam questions in this Orientation folder, or will be very similar to those questions.* The practice list adds which is more likely: chosen from the list is *rare*, similar to it is *typical*, so *do make sure you read the questions in full on exam day*.

:::exam How a question is built
*The actual questions you get will in some cases be paired up with a case study containing a moral dilemma related to computing or may require that you provide your own example related to computing. They may require that you don't just explain something, but also argue for a particular point (e.g. was X right about Y, and why? - provide an example to support your claim).*
:::

Lecture 1 adds one warning about the moral-reasoning question: *Most midterms and final exams include one question on moral reasoning*, it may be a *"trick" question* that asks for something that cannot be done, and *you may also get 0% if it's clear you don't understand validity, soundness, what is a conceptual, moral/value or empirical argument*.

## Answers copied from AI or the web usually miss the question

The practice list is blunt: *the actual questions on the midterm and final exams will not be taken directly from this list. This means that AI answers to these questions will more often than not fail to be relevant for the exams. Using a combination of just AI + passive memorization is likely to hurt you as this approach may give you the illusion that you're prepared.* The in-class definitions also differ from the web's: the course's *objectivism* is not Ayn Rand's, and *absolutism* is Pojman's definition from lecture 3.

That is why every practice item in these modules asks you to **write your answer first**, two paragraphs, then reveal the model and tick the points a marker looks for. Reading a model you have not tried to write gives exactly the *illusion that you're prepared* the instructor warns about.

## Two hours for four answers is about 25 minutes each

| Step | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Read all six questions and choose your four | 5 |
| Four answers, two paragraphs each | 4 × 25 = 100 |
| Re-read: is the position stated, are the definitions in course words, is there an example? | 15 |

If the paper offers five questions you can answer, still write four well rather than five thinly: *answer 4 of 6 options to get your full mark*.
