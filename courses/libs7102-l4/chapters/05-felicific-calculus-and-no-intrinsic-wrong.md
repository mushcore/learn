---
title: Bentham measures pleasure on seven dimensions, so no kind of action is wrong in itself
minutes: 20
---

If pleasure and pain are all that count, and everyone counts equally, you need a way to say how much. Bentham offered one, and with it a striking consequence: no kind of action, lying or stealing included, is wrong in itself.

## The felicific calculus measures pleasure and pain on seven dimensions

Slide 18 is headed *Quantification of pleasure and pain (note that this is pre-Mill utilitarianism and was not kept by Mill)*: *Bentham's felicific calculus, later abandoned by Mill*. *One measures an action's value with respect to pleasure and pain according to the following*:

| Dimension | Slide 18 |
|---|---|
| Intensity | *how strong the pleasure or pain is* |
| Duration | *how long it lasts* |
| Certainty | *how likely the pleasure or pain is to be the result of the action* |
| Proximity | *how close the sensation will be to performance of the action* |
| Fecundity | *how likely it is to lead to further pleasures or pains* |
| Purity | *how much intermixture there is with the other sensation* |
| Extent | *the number of people affected by the action* |

The slide lists the first six together and adds the seventh separately: *One also considers extent*. Slide 19 calls the whole the *felicific/hedonic calculus*.

## You do not have to measure every time

The same slide answers the obvious worry that nobody can run seven measurements before every act: *Bentham: we don't have to always measure. We often know by experience/common sense that e.g. being in a fight for no good reason will be outweighed by the pain/ Lying causes problems / ...* Experience has already done most of the arithmetic. Mill gives the same reply to the lack-of-time objection in lecture 5.

## No type of action is intrinsically right or wrong

Slide 18 ends with its most important line, marked *Crucially*: *no type of action (e.g. stealing, lying) is intrinsically right or wrong. This is a rejection of absolutism. Actions can only be wrong when they fail to be appropriate means to maximizing utility (he approved of autonomy and liberty, but only insofar as they have instrumental value).*

The course notes press the point. Killing can be moral in some cases, such as a soldier defending their country against an invading force, and the same goes for hacking and phishing. In the vast majority of cases these are wrong, so we get used to repeating "X is wrong" and get tricked into thinking we must be absolutists about it. The notes list rejecting absolutism, the rigid "Never lie!", as one of utilitarianism's advantages. Autonomy and liberty get no exemption: they matter only as means.

## Bentham's version in six points

Slide 19 summarizes *Bentham's classical act utilitarianism*:

1. *Psychological hedonism. Highlights the fundamental role of pleasure and pain in human life – the two "sovereign masters" as the only intrinsic motivations*
2. *Normative Hedonism – The only intrinsic good is pleasure - Equates the good with pleasure and the bad with pain.*
3. *We should approve or disapprove of an action on the basis of the amount of utility (pleasure/pain) it produces as a consequence – it is a consequentialist normative theory.*
4. *Pleasure and pain can be quantified (felicific/hedonic calculus)*
5. *Commitment to equal consideration - everyone's pleasure and pain counts equally*
6. *Psychological egoism – we act morally out of self-interest– all our ultimate motivations are self-interested.*

It adds: *Bentham focused on making society work through socially imposed sanctions – he wanted to apply this idea to the laws of England. It helped him to justify laws for punishment, and to limit punishment (Bentham – punishment is in itself an evil, and can only be justified if it is a means to bring greater good).* Punishment causes pain, so it needs a greater good to justify it, which both licenses and limits it.

Points 1 and 6 are two different theories. Slide 20 asks whether you can hold one without the other.

## A hedonist need not be an egoist: Mill is the example

Slide 20's answer: *It is possible. This would be someone who believes that everyone does act out of pleasure all the time (psychological hedonism), but who gets pleasure from satisfying other people's desires or needs. John Stuart Mill is an example of this.* The slide's reminder matters too: a psychological egoist is someone who holds the theory, *this doesn't necessarily say anything about the person's own behaviour, or whether they are selfish or not*. Mill keeps psychological hedonism and drops Bentham's egoism, as [Mill refines Bentham](#/libs7102-l4/mill-refines-bentham) shows.
