## Utilitarianism says Jim should kill one to save nineteen

The verdict follows the three steps of the [utilitarian algorithm](#/libs7102-l4/the-utilitarian-algorithm). Everyone in the square is affected. Accepting leaves one dead and nineteen alive; refusing leaves twenty dead; the gun leaves twenty-one dead. With equal consideration of interests, accepting maximizes utility, and the one man dies in every option. The ends justify the means here: killing an innocent person is right because no available alternative has higher utility.

The notes predict your own answer: *there is a good chance the decision you reach concerning what should be done in the case will be a decision shaped by utilitarian commitments, even if you've never heard of utilitarianism*. Williams himself thought *"the simple-mindedness of utilitarianism disqualifies it totally"*, and Samuel Scheffler observed that *"utilitarianism has gained a reputation for moral clumsiness that is unparalleled among ethical theories."*

On slide 30's second question, utilitarianism answers yes: refusing is a choice with twenty deaths as its actual outcome. Lecture 5 slide 26 turns Williams's doubt into the [strong doctrine of negative responsibility](#/libs7102-l5/aggregation-responsibility-trivial-promises) objection: are we *really responsible for the things we don't do as much as we are responsible for the things that we do*?

## Saying every choice is wrong is the absolutist's trap

The Kill One to Save Nineteen notes: *some will say that killing is wrong (a very popular absolutist claim), and not killing is also wrong (allowing others to die through inaction), so they conclude that whatever someone does in Bernard Williams' thought experiment is morally wrong.* Such conclusions *are related to particular conceptions of ethics that may be mistaken, and there are alternatives.*

The alternative is the one from the prisoners' switch: *the most moral choice is "the moral choice"*, because you had to deliberate to find the best choice available. Jim's outcome is deeply regrettable either way, but *we shouldn't confuse the desirability of the outcome with the morality of the choice.*

The notes add Kant's view, which the course studies later: *only your choice can be right or wrong. For him there are no unethical circumstances.* You stay free to choose among the limited options, *no matter how difficult the choice may be*. If someone grabs your hand and slaps another with it, *this is not your action*, and you cannot be blamed for it.

## Killing is wrong in most cases, not in every case

*But isn't killing wrong?* Jim's case differs from the switch because Jim himself must kill. The notes ask you to *make up your mind about whether you really need to be an absolutist about killing (and other actions)*. Utilitarianism *allows you to say that killing is moral in some cases, such as to save others (e.g. a soldier defending their country against an invading force). The same would go for hacking, phishing, etc. It just so happens that in the vast majority of cases, it's wrong to do these things, and so we get used to repeating the words - X is wrong - and then get tricked into thinking we must be absolutists about it.*

One more trap is *lazy wording*: saying *it is morally wrong to kill, but you need to do it anyway* amounts to saying *it is morally right to do what is morally wrong*.
