---
title: Equal consideration counts everyone's interests equally; it does not treat everyone equally
minutes: 20
---

Once pleasure and pain are what count, you have to say whose. Utilitarianism's answer is everyone's, equally, and the most common exam mistake is to read that as "equal treatment" or "for the majority".

## No one's pleasure or pain counts for more than anyone else's

Slide 15 states the principle in bold: *The pleasure or pain of one person should be of no more value than the pleasure or pain of another.* To apply it, *take a perfectly impartial "view from the universe" – Sidgwick* (the slide's portrait is Henry Sidgwick). The course notes spell it out: an individual's interests are of no more value than anyone else's, regardless of sex, age, race, education and so on. Mill credits Bentham with the rule "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one": you do not count your father's pleasure as more important merely because he is your father.

## Equal consideration is not equality, equal treatment or just distribution

Slide 15 then says what the principle is not:

- *Not a principle of general equality*.
- *Not a principal of equal treatment or just distribution.*
- *The action recommended by applying the ECI may well only benefit a few, or only one.*

And, in bold: *The idea of ECI is: The interests of each person affected by the action must count, and they must count equally, when* measuring *utility.*

Slide 16 tests this with five people and one action. Work it out before reading on.

## The five-person row: three lose, and the action can still be right

Slide 16 puts three lines above the row:

- *ECI = Interests always counted equally*
- *ECI does NOT mean individuals are always* treated *equally*
- *Utilitarianism is not about serving the interests of the "majority"*

| Health worker | Health worker | Health worker | Patient | Police officer |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −2 | −1 | −2 | +500 | +5 |

The total is +500. The three health workers are a majority and each is worse off; their −5 is counted in full, and it is outweighed. Nothing about the result is equal, yet every interest was considered equally. The italic *measuring* on slide 15 is the point: equality belongs to the measuring, not to the outcome. The footnote matters for later: *For the sake of this example and for easier visualization, we're purely quantifying pleasure and pain (assigning values). We'll see that Mill rejects Bentham's strict quantification of utility.*

The course notes make the same point with killing: equal consideration can lead a utilitarian to say it is right to kill one person to save five, which is clearly not equal treatment.

## "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" is an often-misunderstood quote

Slide 17 quotes Bentham (1843): *"Priestley was the first (unless it was Beccaria) who taught my lips to pronounce this sacred truth:- That the greatest happiness of the greatest number is the foundation of morals and legislation."*

The slide corrects the reading at once:

- *This does* not *mean* just *distribution - that utility should be* evenly divided *among the largest number of people possible. Or that we serve the majority.*
- *Only total aggregated utility matters*, in bold.
- *Even though Bentham* personally *thought just distribution is* more likely *to maximize utility, and that we shouldn't sacrifice the rights of one for the greater good - "the interest of everybody is sacred, or the interest of nobody", it's not clear how his* theory *of utilitarianism can satisfy his ideal.*

So the phrase names neither a majority vote nor a fair share. It survives as a slogan, and the course notes record that Bentham's view is often summarized as "the greatest good for the greatest number", but the theory itself maximizes the total. Bentham's own hope that the rights of one would not be sacrificed is exactly what the five-person row and the kill-one-to-save-five case show the theory cannot promise.
