Part II of the activity tests whether utilitarianism captures everything that matters morally. Slide 34 asks it outright: *Does utilitarianism fail to capture all that is morally relevant?* Answer each case before you read the verdict.

## The trolley can be switched from five people onto one

Slide 31, *Trolley problem*, is a drawing: a trolley runs toward five people tied to the track, and a person at a lever can switch it onto a side track where one person is tied.

The verdict matches Jim's: one death instead of five maximizes utility. The week 4 notes add a real case of the same reasoning. In 1940 Churchill learned, through the Turing-designed machine that read Enigma messages, that Coventry would be bombed. To keep the Germans from learning their code was broken, he gave no warning and used the advantage later, at a more decisive moment. *According to utilitarianism, it was morally right to sacrifice the lives of hundreds or thousands of people at Coventry because Churchill's action (or inaction, in this case) ultimately saved the lives of more people.*

To the utilitarian the two trolley cases are one case. If you feel they differ, the feeling tracks something else: using a person as a means, or doing harm with your own hands.

## The same push can cost your best friend to save five war criminals

```quiz

    "model": "If pushing actually maximizes utility, utilitarianism says it is required.     "points": ["applies ECI: the friend counts no more, the war criminals no less", "says pushing is required if it actually maximizes utility", "says only consequences (e.g. the criminals' future harm) could change the verdict, not guilt or friendship as such"],

    "explain": "Answering that the criminals deserve to die appeals to desert, which utilitarianism does not count except through consequences."

```

Equal consideration is what makes this case hard. The notes' own example is *don't count your father's pleasure as more important than that of others merely because he is your father*. Friendship and guilt matter only through what they cause; Lecture 5 asks whether such special ties deserve more, in [Singer, impartiality and supererogation](#/libs7102-l5/singer-impartiality-and-supererogation).

Slide 9 listed the four intuitive distinctions between the hats: *permission/consent*, *legality*, *intention* and *outcome*. *Utilitarianism tells us we should look only at the outcome*, so a grey hat's break-in can be right. Strangelove's case shows the cost: luck, not judgment, made his act right.

Andy's hack also used inside knowledge from a client, the kind of conflict of interest Lecture 2 warns about; utilitarianism still judges your lie only by what followed from it.

Slide 34 closes the list with the claim that ties all four cases together: *According to utilitarianism, these actions are not just advised - they are morally required.* Pushing the fat man, pushing your friend, Strangelove's hack and your lie to the boss are each the right action if no available alternative had higher utility, and then you would be wrong not to do them.

That is the slide's challenge. If you think permission, intention, honesty or friendship matter in their own right, utilitarianism leaves them out, and Lecture 5 builds its objections on that gap, starting with [Mill's three objections](#/libs7102-l5/mills-three-objections).
