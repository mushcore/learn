title: Rejecting relativism leaves open which theory tells you what to do
minutes: 20

Lecture 3 rejected relativism. That says some things have objective value, not which things or what to do in a hard case; a normative theory answers that.

## Judge everyone, every day, without abusing tolerance

Slide 2 is headed *Review – Avoid excessive tolerance*. It restates last week's rule: *judge everyone, every day*. *Judge people in your community and others. Ourselves, friends and strangers.* *Don't passively accept a company's or co-workers' actions and values. Moral judgement is a bit like voting. We don't all get it right, but it's important we all try, and it's our right.*

The slide warns that *appeals to tolerance are often abused*, in two ways:

1. *Calling you out for not being tolerant (when you shouldn't be)*.
2. *False moral equivalence (e.g. the other side does the same, so we can do it / everybody does it so it must be OK)*.

"Every team ships this tracker" is the second abuse.

## Hypermoralism is the opposite failure

Slide 3 adds: *also avoid hypermoralism*. *There are also overly harsh assessments - moral panics associated with both the left and right.* The *outcomes: cancel culture, defending one good value at the cost of all others, polarization*. Its two examples:

- *defense of trans or women's rights leads to banning/boycotting J.K. Rowling, Woody Allen, NAZI science etc*; separating the art from the artist *may involve reading/watching what you already [have] but not buying the art of a living artist*;
- *defense of freedom of speech as trumping all other rights - freedom to say anything racist, sexist, etc / Defense of justice becomes a battle against affirmative action / DEI*.

The rule: *Blanket judgements are typically poor judgements. Make sure you target individual behaviours, rather than rush to conclude a whole person or culture is evil and must be silenced. Keep the dialogue going. Talk to people who disagree with [you].* And in bold: *Tolerance must have limits to be meaningful and useful. Not too little, not too much.*

## Rejecting relativism does not make your values correct

Slide 4's answer is *we don't know*: Gandhi and Hitler were both objectivists. So: *To know what to do we need to look into normative theories of ethics. All reject relativism + provide guidance.* The course studies three, classic act utilitarianism, Kantian ethics and virtue ethics, and *we'll start with utilitarianism*.

## Four positions, one of them relativism

Slide 5 restates lecture 3's positions:

| Position | Slide 5 |
|---|---|
| Normative relativism | *Culture is all that defines right/wrong. We can only judge by reference to the culture (within that relative standard)* |
| Normative objectivism | *Morality does not depend solely on social custom or individual acceptance (i.e. normative relativism is wrong)* |
| Objectivist & absolutist | *Moral objectivism is right + there are principles blind to circumstances (e.g. "You ought to keep your promises", "Hacking is wrong")* |
| Objectivist & non-absolutist | *Moral objectivism is right + there are objective values (e.g. protect the innocent, honesty, loyalty, pleasure - utilitarianism), but they cannot be captured in rules that apply in all cases without regard for circumstances* |

Utilitarianism is the last row.

## "It depends" is not relativism

Slide 6 names a *common mistake*: *stating that relativism means: "It Depends"*. *Don't confuse relativism with objectivism that is non-absolutist (e.g. utilitarianism, virtue ethics). Both can say "It depends"*. They differ on what it depends on:

- *Relativism says that right and wrong depends on the culture/opinion where the judgment is made (I can judge within my culture and according to my culture's values)*.
- *Non-absolutist objectivism says that right or wrong depends on the relevant circumstances of the action. For instance, does killing one maximize utility?*

## Three approaches, in chronology

Slide 10 dates the three approaches the course covers:

| Approach | Thinkers | Focus (slide 10) |
|---|---|---|
| Virtue ethics | Aristotle (385-323 BC) and Hume (1711-1776) | *Focus on character and moral dispositions (virtues). What dispositions constitute a good life?* |
| Kantian ethics | Kant (1724-1804) | *a deontological theory (i.e. based on duty). The focus is on motivation – duty and rationality. Actions have moral worth if they are the right actions and are performed for the right reason (duty).* |
| Classical act utilitarianism | Bentham (1748-1832) and Mill (1806-1873; the portrait on the slide is Mill) | *the focus is on the outcome of an action in terms of pleasure and pain. Locates the moral quality of actions on the actual effect of actions on others.* |

The slide stresses *actual*: the effect that happened, not the one intended. The course notes add Henry Sidgwick (1907) as a third proponent; the course follows Mill's version.
