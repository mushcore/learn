Peter Singer takes utilitarianism at its word on two points most of us resist: a stranger far away counts as much as someone close, and giving to help is a duty, not charity. Slides 34 to 38 follow his "Famine, Affluence, and Morality".

## Singer says helping distant victims is impartial duty, not charity

Slide 34 opens with Singer's question: *Must we help Bengali refugees? What obligations do we in affluent nations have toward victims of natural disasters or other disasters of that magnitude?* His answer has two parts.

- *We must be impartial (emotional proximity and physical distance do not matter to utilitarianism).*
- *It's also not mere charity to help (charity defined as beyond the call of duty - ethical, but not strictly required). It's our duty - definitely required!*

The slide adds that *these ideas can easily be applied to computing*: *You're asked to implement a dark pattern that impacts users in India. How far you go to protect those users? Should you point out to your boss that this in fact a dark pattern? Does it depend on how much is at stake for you?* Users you will never meet count as much as your colleagues.

## The drowning child: prevent the bad unless it costs something of comparable moral significance

The essay's principle: if it is in our power to prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of *comparable moral significance*, we ought to do it. Singer's application is the drowning child: walking past a shallow pond, you see a child drowning; you ought to wade in, though it means muddy clothes, because the muddy clothes are insignificant and the child's death is very bad. Singer gives it right after his qualified form of the principle (prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything morally significant); it works on either version. The principle takes no account of proximity or distance: a neighbour's child ten yards away and a refugee ten thousand miles away are owed the same.

## Singer says we cannot discriminate against someone because they are far away

Slide 35 quotes Singer: *[W]e cannot discriminate against someone merely because he is far away from us*, *in distance or psychologically - friend or stranger*.

Perfect impartiality means your own child counts no more than a stranger. Slide 35 lays out three answers.

- **A1.** *Absolutely. Impartiality is the only way to be moral.* This is Singer's and classical utilitarianism's verdict: save the two strangers, and the eleven.
- **A2.** *Possible objection against utilitarianism: "No ought without can". Almost no one can be that impartial. So there's no obligation to be that impartial.*
- **A3.** *Possible objection against utilitarianism: It's actually wrong to be that impartial. There are special duties to those who rely on us (e.g. our children, partner, friends).*

A2 says the demand is impossible; A3 says it is wrong even if possible. The course notes put A3 as a failure *to recognise special bonds between individuals*, done *in the name of equity*.

## Singer's strong version is the correct one, he says, but he argues from the moderate

Slide 36 quotes the essay: *It is beyond the scope of my argument to consider whether the distinction [between duty and charity] should be redrawn or abolished altogether*. The slide glosses it: *In other words, Singer proposes to change or eliminate the threshold of supererogation. He seems to propose here the more reasonable redrawing.* His two versions:

- **Strong:** *prevent bad things from happening unless in doing so we would be sacrificing something of comparable moral significance*. It *does seem to require reducing ourselves to the level of marginal utility*, giving until more giving would cost us as much as it relieves. *The strong version seems to me to be the correct one.*
- **Moderate:** *prevent bad occurrences unless, to do so, we had to sacrifice something morally significant*.

## Redrawing the threshold creates a duty of charity; abolishing it erases charity

Slide 37 states both outcomes. *If it's redrawn, then we need to speak of a moral duty to do some charity.* *If it's abolished, no duty is considered supererogatory (above and beyond the call of duty) - note that this what classical act utilitarianism actually calls for!* Abolishing it, which *Singer and classical act utilitarianism say is ultimately right*, means *we can no longer distinguish between* duty (*ethically mandatory*) and charity (*moral to give, but optional*). Since the right action is the one with the highest utility, it is always required.

Slide 38 asks: *Do we move it up (moderate proposal) or eliminate it (utilitarianism proper)?*
