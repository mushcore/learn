---
title: "Mill answers three objections: the doctrine of swine, too high for humanity, lack of time"
minutes: 30
---

Slide 11 lists *the three objections to utilitarianism as discussed by Mill himself*: the doctrine of swine, too high for humanity, and lack of time. Each reply works, and each time the next slide asks at what cost.

## The doctrine of swine says a life aimed at pleasure is fit only for pigs

Slide 12 gives the objection *as described by Mill*: *"To suppose that life has no higher end than pleasure – no better and nobler object of desire and pursuit – they designate as utterly mean and grovelling; as a doctrine worthy only of swine"*. *The objection is based on utilitarianism's claim that pleasure is the only thing that has intrinsic value (all other things have instrumental value).* If pleasure is the only good, a pig eating all day lives the best life, and morality shrinks to a mindless search for pleasure (the slide shows a sleeping pig).

## Mill replies that pleasures differ in quality, judged by those who know both

Slide 13: *Mill: "some kinds of pleasure are more desirable and more valuable than others" – so we must consider quality as well as quantity.* This is Mill's break with Bentham's quantity-only calculus ([lecture 4](#/libs7102-l4/mill-refines-bentham)): *"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied, better Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied"*. *How to know which are more desirable? Ask those who are properly acquainted with both, says Mill (the "competent judges").* Lecture 4 slide 25 gives Mill's test: *Of two pleasures, if there be one to which all or almost all who have experience of both give a decided preference, irrespective of any feeling of moral obligation to prefer it, that is the more desirable pleasure.*

## Three doubts hang over the quality reply

Slide 13 then asks *Is this a good response?*

- *How do we know who is a competent judge (who is properly acquainted with the two pleasures to compare)?*
- *What if judges disagree?*
- *Doesn't the idea of quality possibly smuggle non-utilitarian forms of morality evaluation to utilitarianism?*

The course notes add that the reply fills an important gap but makes utilitarianism *a moving target for critics*, able to adjust itself ad hoc through the judges, and brings in a high degree of subjectivity.

## Too high for humanity says no one acts for the general interest of society

Slide 14 gives *Mill's description of the objection*: *"It is exacting (demanding) too much to require that people shall always act from the inducement of promoting the general interests of society"*. The slide's example: *When we are kind to a neighbour, are we really motivated by the general interest of society? Surely, this cannot be the case. It is demanding too much.*

## Mill replies that the objection confuses the rule of action with its motive

Slide 15: *Mill says that this is confusing the "rule of action with the motive of it. It is the business of ethics to tell us what are our duties, or by what test we may know them, but no system of ethics requires that the sole motive of all we do shall be a feeling of duty"*. The test of right is the consequences; in the course notes' words, any motivation will do so long as society benefits. Then the slide's question: *But is it true that motives don't matter?* Slide 16, *Is it true that motives don't matter in establishing right and wrong?*, tests it with a case from a Michael Sandel lecture.

On the theory's own test, doctors A and B did equally wrong and doctor C did right, because only the actual outcome counts. Most of us say C acted worst and A was merely unlucky. The course notes call Mill's clarification *deeply counter-intuitive*: it does not address whether a meaningful ethical theory can leave motivation out. The utilitarian's best answer is that the theory judges actions, not the character of the people who do them.

## Lack of time says there is no time to calculate before acting

Slide 17 gives *Mill's description of the objection*: *"There is not time, previous to action, for calculating and weighing the effects of any line of conduct on the general happiness"*.

## Mill replies that experience has already taught us the effects of actions

Slide 18: *"There is no difficulty in proving any ethical standard whatever to work ill if we suppose universal idiocy to be conjoined with it; but on any hypothesis after that, mankind must by this time have acquired positive beliefs as to the effects of some actions on their happiness"*. *Translation: any normative ethical theory can be accused to fail to provide correct answers if we assume the person using it is an idiot. Let's not assume that.* *One can know, by experience, that some actions (e.g. lying, corruption, torture) are likely to cause pain, other to cause pleasure. There is no need to calculate every time. Bentham had already made that point.* The course notes add that when there is time you can do better than these rules of thumb and calculate more carefully. The rules of thumb are how you decide; what makes the act right is still its actual consequences ([the utilitarian algorithm](#/libs7102-l4/the-utilitarian-algorithm)).
