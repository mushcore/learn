Lecture 5 ends by turning the objections on you: if you still want to be a utilitarian, slide 39 says, *you should have responses to the objections raised*. Slide 40 lists fourteen, and an exam answer about any of them must name the objection, the slide's example and a reply.

## Slide 39 asks four questions of anyone who stays a utilitarian

1. *Consider the objections we discussed* (the list on slide 40).
2. *Do you have responses for each? Identify the most problematic objection, and come up with an example to illustrate how its problematic.*
3. *In light of your responses, ask yourself whether you are a utilitarian?*
4. *Can we entirely give up on considerations of social utility? (given enough social utility, does it ever tip the scale, where we should be prepared to engage in some actions even if they seem to violate individual rights?)*

The fourth question cuts the other way. Rejecting utilitarianism does not mean consequences never matter; it asks whether, with enough at stake, utility can still outweigh a right.

The first three are the ones Mill himself answered; the other eleven are slide 19's additional problems. Many share one root: utility is summed and maximized, so rights, promises, fairness and special bonds count only as pleasure and pain in the total.

## Feeding chosen numbers into the calculation is not being a utilitarian

The course notes end with a warning: *You may believe you are a utilitarian based on a misunderstanding*. Hard decisions need some comparison of what is gained and lost, but *the necessity of coming up with a decision based on some sort of comparison does not necessarily mean you need to accept utilitarianism*.

Their example is commercial airplane software: *How reliable must a commercial airplane software be?* On one side, lives are at stake: the pleasures of being alive, those missed if someone dies, and the pain to those who miss them. On the other, there is *pleasure in paying less for a ticket*, and pleasure from the airline doing well if it spends a little less on development and testing, such as better-paid employees.

One could try to rescue the calculation by assigning human life the "appropriate" utility. The notes ask whether that person is still using the calculation to find out what is right. *If you are a utilitarian, it is the calculation that tells you what is right.* If instead you already know the answer, that *human lives cannot be traded for a little more profit*, and pick the values so the result comes out that way, then *maybe you are not a utilitarian... You are just shaping utilitarianism to fit your expectations.*

:::exam The test of a utilitarian
The theory's verdict must come from the sum. An answer that adjusts the numbers until utilitarianism agrees with intuition has quietly used another standard.
:::
