Slide 19 lists the problems Mill never answered. Four of them follow from the same two features, adding everyone's utility into one sum and always maximizing it: aggregation, negative responsibility, trivial actions and promises.

## Aggregation adds everyone's utility into one total

Step 2 of the slide 8 procedure aggregates *all relevant utility* for each option, with equal consideration of interests, on the actual outcome. Slide 25 asks what that sum costs: *Is it OK to aggregate all utility? Or must we respect the rights of individuals, even at a cost to general happiness?*

An individual's right counts only as one more quantity of pleasure or pain in the total, so a large enough sum on the other side always wins. Act utilitarianism's verdict on slide 25's question: the privacy violation was ethical, because its actual outcome added up to more utility. The user's right to privacy was never weighed as a right, only as his displeasure, and the RCMP conviction outweighed it. The course notes draw the general lesson: summing utility *may end up typically serving a majority and not respecting minority rights* (often, not always: slide 6 says utilitarianism does not maximize for the majority, only the total, which in many cases happens to favour larger groups), and it may make it *morally acceptable (indeed, required), to lie, break promises, steal, torture*. A utilitarian can reply with Mill's quality of pleasures, but then, the notes warn, highly subjective judgments are smuggled into the calculation.

## Williams says you are not as responsible for what you allow as for what you do

Recall Jim and Pedro from [Lecture 4](#/libs7102-l4/jim-and-the-twenty): if Jim shoots one villager the other nineteen go free; if he refuses, Pedro kills all twenty. Utilitarianism has a strong doctrine of negative responsibility: if Jim refuses, he is as much to blame for the twenty deaths as if he had caused them. Slide 26: Bernard Williams *asks whether we are really responsible for the things we don't do as much as we are responsible for the things that we do*. He is *not denying that one can be guilty of negligence in certain contexts*.

His integrity argument: *One may have vowed never to kill. Is not killing as blameworthy as killing?* Integrity places *the blame entirely or at least primarily on Pedro rather than oddly placing it on the person who refuses to hurt anyone out of moral integrity*. The course notes call the utilitarian demand an *implausible demandingness*: it requires an impartial point of view Williams thinks impossible, and it may require you to kill or torture to stop someone else.

Slide 26 then extends the case. Pedro releases the twenty, gets another twenty, and asks Jim for *increasingly horrible actions, again hurting 1 to save 19*. *The utilitarian will always make the same calculation, and will keep always doing what Pedro expects*.

## Maximizing utility leaves no room for trivial actions

Slide 27: *utility must be maximized*, which *seems to entail that we can't take time out. Ever.* Buying new shoes, watching Netflix or going for a walk is wrong whenever some other available action, such as volunteering, yields more utility.

## A promise binds only when keeping it maximizes utility

Slide 28 gives the utilitarian view of promises:

- *Promises will rarely be kept, since you only need to keep a promise when it maximizes utility.*
- *When a promise is kept, there's no requirement to do it because it was promised.*
- *Breaking a promise may cause pain* (and that pain counts), *but otherwise there's no intrinsic moral obligation to keep promises.*

Slide 29 takes a case from Sandel's lecture, and slide 30 gives the utilities.

| Option | Utility |
|---|---|
| Use body for bait | +25 |
| Bury body, according to promise | −10 |
| Let body rot where it is | −2 |
| Throw body in sea | −1 |

The utilitarian verdict: no obligation. Bait has the highest utility, so it is the right action, and burying the body as promised is wrong. Slide 30's other two questions get the same treatment. Whether he *should have promised in the first place* depends only on the actual consequences of promising: comforting a dying man at no cost, since the promise need not be kept, may well have maximized utility. Whether *the grandfather* should *have asked him to make that promise, knowing the consequences for his grandson* is judged the same way, by what the request actually caused. The promise itself adds nothing to either verdict.
