## Psychological hedonism explains action; normative hedonism justifies it

Lecture 4 slide 13 drew the line. *Psychological/motivational hedonism: Pleasure and pain are the ultimate explanations for action.* *Normative hedonism: Pleasure is the only intrinsic good, pain the only intrinsic bad.* Mill dropped Bentham's psychological egoism but kept both hedonisms (course notes). Slide 20, *Psychological and Normative Hedonism*, questions each:

- *Is it really true that all our actions can be ultimately explained by reference to pleasure?*
- *Is it really true that all our actions can be ultimately justified by reference to pleasure?* The slide's gloss, *Is it true that we should act to maximize pleasure, because pleasure is the only good?*, expects the answer no.

Between the two questions the slide sets a test: *Is my business/romantic partner cheating on me?* If pleasure explained and justified everything, you would rather not know; most people want to know, though the truth hurts. *One famous argument/answer against both: Nozick's experience machine*, which starts from this premise: *If pleasure and pain are the only things that have ultimate value, you should want them even if they are not deserved/based on real accomplishments/etc.* Slide 20's picture is a Roman feast; slide 21 shows *The Experience Machine by Yescela Vorazan*, a child strapped into a *Happiness Machine*.

## Nozick's machine gives you any life you want, for good

Slide 22: Robert Nozick, *Anarchy, State and Utopia (1974)*. The experience machine:

1. *Can give you any experiences you want*
2. *Perfectly simulates a life of achievement, pleasure, love, etc*
3. *It is indistinguishable from reality*
4. *You must plug in permanently (you won't remember choosing)*

*Do you plug in?*

## Nozick gives three reasons not to plug in

Slide 23 explains the refusal:

1. *We want to do certain things, and not just have the experience of doing them.* *"It is only because we first want to do the actions that we want the experiences of doing them."* (Nozick, 43). The gloss: *it's not the experience – the pleasure – that matters the most*; applied to the slide's example, *we want the person to be well, and secondarily to have pleasure because they are doing well*.
2. *We want to be a certain sort of person.* *"Someone floating in a tank is an indeterminate blob."* (Nozick, 43). *Character matters to us, rather than just pleasure – whether we are truly a good person, or just falsely believe we are a good person.*
3. *Plugging into an experience machine limits us to a man-made reality (it limits us to what we can make).* *"There is no actual contact with any deeper reality, though the experience of it can be simulated."* (Nozick, 43). *It seems truth matters to us – ignorance may be bliss, but it's worthless bliss.*

*Conclusion: pleasure is not the ultimate motivation + pleasure is not the only thing that has intrinsic value.* The one machine counts against both hedonisms at once.

## Mill's proof reads "desirable" as "desired"

Lecture 4 slide 26: Bentham had said the principle of utility *"does not admit of direct proof"*, and Mill *ambitiously thought he could provide this proof*: *"The only proof capable of being given that an object is visible is that people actually see it.... In like manner, I apprehend, the sole evidence it is possible to produce that anything is desirable is that people do actually desire it."* Slide 24 names the view behind it, the *Desire-fulfillment theory of welfare*: what we desire is what is good for us, a broader view than pleasure alone (course notes). It sums the proof up as *Mill – We desire pleasure, therefore it is desirable*, and gives *2 interpretations for "desirable"*:

- *We desire pleasure, therefore we desire pleasure – trivially true but circular.*
- *We desire pleasure, therefore it is good – clearly wrong.*

*Mill argued each individual is better positioned than anyone else to know what is good for himself/herself. This is one argument he used to refuse interference from the state in personal matters that don't affect society.*

The objections: *don't humans desire all sorts of things that are bad for them?* *If I desire to be hurt simply because I have low self-esteem, is it desirable that I am hurt? No.* *If I desire crack because I'm addicted to crack, does that show that crack is desirable (good for me)? No.*
