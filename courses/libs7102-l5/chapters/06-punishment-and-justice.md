---
title: Punishment and justice have only derivative value for a utilitarian
minutes: 30
---

Equal consideration of interests sounds like a theory of justice. Slides 31 to 33 show it is not: punishment is right for a utilitarian only when it happens to maximize utility, and slide 33 then tests fair pay.

## Punishment produces utility in three ways

Bentham had already called punishment *in itself an evil*, justified only *if it is a means to bring greater good* ([Lecture 4](#/libs7102-l4/bentham-and-the-principle-of-utility)). Slide 31 names the goods it can bring: *Punishment produces utility through* rehabilitation, deterrence and vengeance. Rehabilitation makes the offender better; deterrence makes others afraid to offend; vengeance pleases those who wanted the offender to suffer. Each counts only as utility in the sum.

## Each source of utility can recommend an unjust punishment

Slide 32 gives the problem with each.

- **Rehabilitation** is *not seen as a right (good in itself) from the point of view of utilitarianism. If we happen to maximize utility by leaving the person e.g. in solitary confinement for life, so be it.*
- **Deterrence**: *Why not frame an innocent and punish him in public? Or convict three people, to make sure the one guilty person is punished?* The public sees a punishment either way, and the deterrent effect is the same.
- **Vengeance** *can cause pleasure, so any punishment that gives pleasure to enough people is morally justified. Whether the person committed the crime or not is not intrinsically morally relevant for the theory.*

## Justice and rights are never values in themselves

The slide closes with the source of all three: *All these problems derive from not seeing justice and a defense of human rights as values in themselves. Acting on them will have only derivative value when considering utility (e.g. protect someone's freedom of speech if doing that maximizes utility in one particular instance).* Derivative value is value borrowed from the utility an act produces. A guilty verdict, a fair sentence or a protected right is right in one case and wrong in the next, depending only on the actual total. This is the same feature that made the RCMP photos and the dying man's promise come out as they did in [the previous lesson](#/libs7102-l5/aggregation-responsibility-trivial-promises).

Slide 33's answer: *For utilitarianism, this should be done if it maximizes utility. But if Peter is sad unless he gets 60% of the amount, this would factor in the calculation.* The reason has two parts: *Equal consideration of interests, but... Utility is aggregated.* Sarah's interests count exactly as much as Peter's, but once both are added into one total, the distribution between them does not matter. A 60-40 split can win, and Peter's resentment, not his work, earns him the extra ten percent. Equal consideration of interests is not equal shares. This is Lecture 4's five-person row again: equal consideration is a rule for counting, not for sharing.
