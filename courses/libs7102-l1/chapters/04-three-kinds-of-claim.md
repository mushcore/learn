title: "Claims are empirical, value or conceptual, and a value conclusion needs a value premise"

The moral-reasoning question asks you to type each claim in an argument, and to spot an argument that tries to reach a moral conclusion from facts alone. Slides 27 to 30 give the three tests and the shape a valid value argument must have.

## A value claim cannot be settled by sensory tests alone

Slide 27 defines the first two kinds. A *value claim* *claims that something has value (e.g. it's good, it's bad, should be done, should not be done) – in a way that cannot be settled by using sensory tests alone*. An *empirical claim*: *if we can use sensory tests alone to determine the truth of the claim*. The slide tests the line with three sentences.

## A conceptual claim is settled by the meaning of the words alone

Slide 28 gives all three tests:

- *Empirical claims - Can we use sensory tests alone to determine the truth of the claim?*
- *Value claims – Is there a judgment of approval/disapproval that cannot be reduced to an empirical claim? Moral and aesthetic claims are examples of types of value claims.*
- *Conceptual claims - Can we use the meaning of the words alone to determine the truth of the claim?*

Typing a claim says nothing about whether it is true. The course notes on reasoning about value stress it: "The moon is made of green cheese" is a false empirical claim, and "There is life on one of the moons of Jupiter" is empirical even though no one can make the observation yet. "Hitler was an evil man" is very likely true but not a factual claim: the value term "evil" makes it a value claim.

## A value argument has a value premise, an empirical premise and a value conclusion

Slide 29 gives Aristotle's shape: *Value premise: All humans should exercise. Empirical premise: I am a human being. Conclusion: I should exercise.* *A value argument contains at least one value claim (often associated with use of words "should/ought"), one of more empirical claims, and a value conclusion.* *In other words: it is not possible to get to a value conclusion by using only empirical statements – "no ought from is".* *Don't be fooled by attempts to get to value conclusions by using empirical statements.*

The course notes call this shape the practical syllogism. Their example: "Strip mining will substantially alter the way of life of people in the Kootenay region, so strip mining in the Kootenays is undesirable" does not follow, since someone could accept the fact and deny the judgment. Adding the value principle "It is undesirable to substantially alter the way of life of people in the Kootenays" makes it valid.

Slide 30's verdict: *The argument is incomplete and is therefore invalid... Lies do hurt the competitors, sure, but that's just an empirical fact. It wasn't established that hurting people is wrong. So the consequence doesn't follow.* *A complete (valid) argument is therefore: P1 Lies hurt people (empirical). P2 There is no moral justification for hurting competitors to get ahead unfairly (value claim). C: Lying is wrong in this case (borrowed value from P2).* The conclusion's "wrong" is *borrowed* from the value premise; it cannot come from anywhere else.

So the course notes' three checks on any value argument: are the empirical and conceptual claims true; is the value principle acceptable; and does the conclusion follow?
