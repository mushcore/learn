Why does ethics need philosophy, now that we have science? Slides 7 to 15 answer, then give the three basics every moral argument rests on.

## Science takes over a question once empirical research can deal with it

Slide 8: *In some cases, philosophy just makes problems more tractable. What is life? Why do things move? In many cases, sciences arose from the work of philosophers (e.g. Aristotle developed the basis of biology, mechanics and economics).* *Once empirical research can deal with it, then science takes over.* But *questions remain that cannot be settled by empirical research alone. "Is it wrong to lie to my boss? Why?"* That does not mean anything goes: the course notes say arguments can be shown to be wrong, and the difference from science is a matter of degree of certainty.

Slide 9 names the branches by their questions: epistemology (*What is knowledge?*), logic (*What constitutes valid reasoning?*), metaphysics (*What is time? Is there causation?*) and ethics (*What makes an action morally right?*).

## Ethics divides into meta-ethics, normative ethics and applied ethics

Slide 10 gives each branch with an example question.

| Branch | What it does (slide 10) | The slide's example |
|---|---|---|
| Meta-ethics | *discusses the meaning of terms and statements* | *What do we mean when we say that an action is "wrong"? Are we just expressing a subjective feeling (e.g. this action disgusts me – "yuck"), or are we perhaps making a more objective statement?* |
| Normative ethics | *explores what moral norms one should follow* | *Is it wrong to lie? In some cases or all cases? Why?* |
| Applied ethics | *examines what one ought to do in particular cases* | *Is there a moral duty to protect Hannah's private data? If so, is this a duty to her because she's a friend, or to all humans?* |

This is an applied ethics course, but it covers all three, because you study a theory before you apply it.

## Philosophy is rational argument, not personal opinion

Slide 11: *Philosophy is much less subjective than what you may think. Think of how arguments are presented in a court of law.* You are marked *on the strength of those arguments and knowledge of the theories and concepts you use to construct them*. *The point is not to express your personal opinions or share your feelings (e.g. "I believe that X", "I don't feel that's right").*

Slide 12 sets common sense against ethical theory. *Arguments strictly from common sense/ based on intuition rely on shared values and are often poorly justified or not justified at all. Intuitions may be wrong.* Intuition stays, as the test of theories: *Is a recommended action and its justification intuitive or not?* The slide then gives a case.

## Three basics govern every moral argument

Slide 14 lists them.

1. *There is "no ought without can": there can be no moral requirement to do something unless this is possible.* The slide's photo of drowning people asks *"Who wants to be rescued first?"*
2. *"No ought from is" – Moral value (what ought to be done) cannot ever be established from empirical facts (what is) alone. Consequence: every moral argument must build on a mutual acceptance of some moral value.* This becomes the value-argument rule in [three kinds of claim](#/libs7102-l1/three-kinds-of-claim).
3. *Psychological explanation is not the same as moral justification (e.g. "I drove over your foot because I thought that would be funny").* Explanations give *psychological/motivational reasons for action (e.g. "I stabbed him because I was in the mood for murder"), but not necessarily good/moral ones.*
