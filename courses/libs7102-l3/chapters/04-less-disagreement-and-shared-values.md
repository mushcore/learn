---
title: "Cultures disagree less than they seem to and share core values"
minutes: 20
---

The cultural differences argument assumes that cultures really do disagree about values. Rachels's next slides question even that, and then answer the people who still hesitate to criticize another culture.

## Much moral disagreement is about empirical facts

Slide 20, *2.5 There is less disagreement than it seems*: *It seems strange that Hindus in India don't eat cows. But why don't they eat cows? Because they believe in reincarnation, so the cow could be someone's dead grandmother. Would you eat your grandmother?* Both sides hold the same value: do not eat your grandmother. *It sounds like we just disagree whether the cow can be someone's grandmother (note this now becomes just a disagreement about empirical claims, rather than one that is truly about moral values).* *Rachels' conclusion: moral disagreement is often for the most part superficial – about empirical fact only.*

This is lecture 1's distinction at work: separate the empirical premise (the cow may be a reincarnated relative) from the value premise (do not eat your relatives), and the disagreement turns out to be about the first.

## All cultures share some values, because they solve social problems

Slide 21, *2.6 All cultures have some values in common*: *we should expect that all cultural groups will protect their infants because:*

1. *Infants are helpless and cannot survive*
2. *If a group did not take care of their young, the group would die out*
3. *Therefore, any existing group will be found to take care of their young.*

The same goes for other values: *Imagine a society where no value is placed on truth-telling. Imagine a society in which there is no loyalty to friends. Imagine a society in which there is no prohibition of murder.* *Unlikely! If it did happen, would it be a good society?* The conclusion: *This suggests moral values are connected to correct solutions to social problems. This grounds the values in objective fact.* A team in which no one can trust a status report, or a market in which no one can trust a signature, does not last.

## We can ask whether a practice promotes welfare, but facts alone do not settle it

Slide 22, *2.7 Judging a cultural practice to be undesirable*. Rachels on female genital mutilation: *"We may ask whether the practice promotes or hinders the welfare of the people whose lives are affected by it"*. *This question may have an answer that could be objectively right or wrong, and not just a matter of personal opinion.*

The slide warns that *this conclusion is commonly misinterpreted by students. Getting your values right is not just a matter of carefully considering the facts objectively*:

- *A) We don't necessarily share the same definition of "welfare". Welfare is a complex concept including both empirical and value components*
- *B) Someone could potentially accept the objective medical (empirical) facts but insist that FGM is physically harmful but still promotes moral welfare*

Rachels sees this: *"the welfare of its members is a value internal to all viable cultures."* The slide calls that a *good job from Rachels trying to argue he's not building an invalid ought from is argument*: the value premise (welfare matters) is one every viable culture already holds, so the argument has its value premise and is not an "ought from is".

## Reluctance to criticize has three answers

Slide 25: *Many are still reluctant to criticize*. They might say, and the slide answers:

| They say | The answer |
|---|---|
| *We should not interfere with the cultural customs of others (post-colonial guilt)* | *It is possible to judge without interfering (e.g. enforcing laws, enacting sanctions)* |
| *We should be tolerant* | *We should be tolerant, but should we be tolerant of all religions and all cultures equally?* |
| *We should not express contempt for another culture* | *We don't have to reject a religion or culture as a whole - just individual practices that should be changed (thoughtful members of religions and cultures actually do this themselves)* |

Judging is moral evaluation; interfering is a further step, which may or may not be right. These answers carry into the tolerance questions of [lesson 6](#/libs7102-l3/tolerance-without-relativism).
