title: "\"Do not judge\" defeats itself, and hypocrisy does not make a judgment wrong"

"Who are you to judge?" is the most common reply to a moral criticism. Slides 11 to 14 show why the reply is a fallacy, and why the judging is the part worth keeping.

## Two lines from Matthew, taken literally, are wrong

Slide 11 names *one source of this dangerous idea in Western culture*: two lines of Matthew 7:1-5. It adds: *Note that this is not a criticism of Judeo-Christian values as a whole! Just these points – if taken literally, they are wrong.*

- *Don't judge / Judge like you want to be judged*: *"Do not judge, or you too will be judged. For in the same way you judge others, you will be judged, and with the measure you use, it will be measured to you."*
- *Don't judge others as you're not perfect / Judge yourself first*: *"Why do you look at the speck of sawdust in your brother's eye and pay no attention to the plank in your own eye? … You hypocrite, first take the plank out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye."*

## The appeal to hypocrisy is a fallacy because two independent issues are confused

Slide 12: *The idea that one needs moral high ground to acquire the authority to make true claims about morality is mistaken.* It rests on an **appeal to hypocrisy**: *"You are hypocritical, therefore your claim can be ignored"*. *But "appeal to hypocrisy" is a well understood fallacy (a logically invalid argument)*, because *these are two entirely distinct and independent issues (i.e. they don't affect each other)*:

- A) *whether an action is morally wrong or not*
- B) *whether the person making a moral claim/judgement acts in accordance with their own judgement*

The premise is about B, the conclusion about A, so the truth of the premise cannot guarantee the conclusion. In lecture 1's terms, the argument is invalid.

## Pretend it is not a fallacy, and the result is absurd

Slide 13 runs a *reductio ad absurdum*: suppose the appeal to hypocrisy is not a fallacy and follow the consequences.

Slide 13's verdict: *If appeal to hypocrisy were not a fallacy, we would not be able to know whether Hannah is correct without knowing more about her. Has she stolen? Has she ever lied? Has she killed anyone in the past? If she is not pure of heart, then she has no right to say that murder is wrong!* And worse: *If you have lied once in your life (and everyone has!), then you can never say that lying is wrong, etc.* A rule that silences everyone about lying cannot be a rule about who may judge.

## Judging is the part Hannah does right

Slide 14 grants what is true in the criticism of judgement: *If by criticizing "judgement" it is meant that harsh judgement and/or hypocritical judgement is problematic, it's a good point in one sense*: it is unfair to *criticize others too harshly*, and *you should look at your own behaviour too*. *But the presence of hypocrisy doesn't by itself undermine judgement (i.e. moral evaluation of actions).*

Suppose Hannah both *A) steals money and murders people* and *B) judges that stealing is wrong and murder is horrible*. There is a contradiction. *The solution, however, is not to say that Hannah must stop judging! The judging is the part she is doing right!* She should stop stealing and murdering.

The same holds at work. A manager who pads her own resume can still be right that a candidate's padded resume is dishonest; the candidate's resume is no more honest because the manager's is not. Slide 9's five reasons for not judging (fallibility, disagreement, unknown motives, tolerance, hypocrisy) each warn you to judge carefully, not to stop.
