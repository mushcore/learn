---
title: "An objectivist can be tolerant; a relativist cannot defend tolerance"
minutes: 25
---

Relativism looks like the ally of tolerance: if no culture is wrong, we must tolerate them all. Two practice questions test whether that is so, and the slides say it is not.

## Tolerance is a value, and relativism does not follow from it

Slide 35 asks *Can I be an objectivist and still value tolerance?* and answers **Yes!**

- *Many who claim to be normative ethical relativists falsely believe that relativism is a logical consequence of the value of tolerance.*
- *It's not. Should we be tolerant of everything? No.*
- *Each objectivist seems to commit to some core group of values (not necessarily the right ones, and they may or may not be shared with others) where tolerance seems inappropriate (e.g. sexual harassment, fraud, corruption, murder). Only outside this core tolerance is meaningful.*
- *In practice, those who claim to be normative ethical relativists are not consistent. They will probably judge the actions of Hitler or Jeffrey Dahmer.*

The slide draws one's core values as a box inside a wider band labelled *Tolerance*: firm inside, flexible outside.

## The course notes: relativism is less compatible with tolerance than it seems

The course notes take this further. A relativist who meets an intolerant person is in *the awkward or even contradictory position of being required by their own position to be tolerant of intolerance*. Is there *a right to steal intellectual property or a right to sign a contract with the intention to violate its terms as soon as possible*? A relativist who refuses to criticize anyone, *by subscribing to this extreme version of tolerance*, ends up *giving up on every other value*. Tolerance *in a more reasonable, measured sense* is *more compatible with moral objectivism than with moral relativism*.

The notes give an objectivist's version of flexibility: hanging up without saying goodbye is rude in some cultures and fine in others. There is a universal requirement to be polite, with room for interpretation of what it means: *some room for tolerance - but not infinite flexibility, as relativism would have it*.

## Tolerance must be just right, and judging is not interfering

Slide 6 already set the target: *hypermoralism (demanding purity and perfect alignment, no tolerance) and moral relativism (too much tolerance) are serious problems*, and *we need to get tolerance just right*. Slide 25 gives the tools for doing so: *It is possible to judge without interfering*; we ask *should we be tolerant of all religions and all cultures equally?*; and *we don't have to reject a religion or culture as a whole - just individual practices that should be changed*.

## "Do not criticize other cultures" is itself a universal ought

Slide 11 showed that "do not judge", taken literally, is wrong; it is also a judgment. The same structure sits inside relativism's own claim 6 (slide 17): *It is arrogance to try to judge the conduct of other peoples. We should be tolerant.* Claims 4 and 5 say there are *no moral truths that hold for all peoples at all times* and that *the moral code of a society determines what is right in that society*.
