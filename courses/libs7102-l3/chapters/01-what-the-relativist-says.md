When two sides of a conflict each call their cyberattacks justified, can an outsider say one is wrong? The relativist says no.

## Microsoft disrupted Strontium, and the relativist asks why anyone should interfere

Slide 2: in 2022 Microsoft wrote, *"We recently observed attacks targeting Ukrainian entities from Strontium, a Russian GRU-connected actor we have tracked for years. This week, we were able to disrupt some of Strontium's attacks on targets in Ukraine."* *A moral relativist may ask: "Why should North Americans interfere? Isn't it all a matter of perspective?"*

## The relativist makes two claims

Slide 3 gives the view: *"From the point of view of each culture, each is right and there's no universal/objective point of view"*. A: *"From the point of view of Ukraine, Russia is invading, so a cyber attack on Russia is justified"*. B: *"From the point of view of Russia, Ukraine is getting friendly with NATO, so a military operation and a cyber attack on Ukraine are justified"*. Each is correct in its own cultural environment: *morality is real but relative to one's culture, social group, or even purely personal point of view*. So:

1. *Many contrasting views can all be correct without contradiction*
2. *We can judge, but not across cultures. Moral judgments are not universal*

## Relativist arguments are real, not a strawman

Is relativism a strawman? Slide 5: *Even though full-on moral relativists may be rare, relativist thought and speech is real and a problem.* Its two examples:

- **Content moderation.** A global platform censors far-right speech in Germany (*"It's harmful propaganda"*) and drops all moderation in the US (*"We must follow American values of freedom and free expression!"*).
- **Algorithmic fairness.** A hiring AI is trained differently in two countries: one mandates race and gender-based affirmative action, the other sees *considering gender and race* as *enforcing discrimination*.

## Hypermoralism and relativism are both problems

Is hypermoralism the real problem, and tolerance the cure? Slide 6: *No, both hypermoralism (demanding purity and perfect alignment, no tolerance) and moral relativism (too much tolerance) are serious problems and both can connect to polarization.* **We need to get tolerance just right.** Politics matters *because clearly political views affect ethical views*.

Slides 7 and 8 find both failures on the left and on the right: *puritanical hypermoralistic intolerance* (*"Be with us 100% now or be cancelled"*), and relativist arguments *to muddy the waters* (*Russia is just doing what the US does in South America*; Trump's *"very fine people on both sides"*). The two connect: *"You have no tolerance for the AFD" is a way to enforce hypermoralism on freedom of speech* (slide 8).

## "It's wrong to judge" is tempting, and wrong

Slide 9: relativism is *sometimes summarized as: "It's wrong to judge!" (other cultures)*. But *most relativists can consistently judge others, just not judge outside their culture*; only the *culture of one* (*"I live by my own rules!"*) judges no one. The reasons people give for not judging:

- *Our judgements of others are fallible*
- *There is disagreement about the norms of conventional morality*
- *We cannot fully know someone else's motivations for acting*
- *We should be tolerant*
- *Those who judge are not perfect themselves! So it's hypocritical to judge*

*Some conclude: "We should never judge others!"* **This may seem like a tempting view, but it is wrong.**

## Rejecting relativism is a duty, but it does not tell you what to do

Slide 10 sums up. Lecture 1: is there genuine moral action? *Yes.* Lecture 2: convention? *No. Convention can get it wrong.* Lecture 3: so don't judge? *Wrong. We must reject relativism. It's our moral duty to judge (i.e. morally evaluate – and possibly do something about it).* But: *the simple act of rejecting relativism … still won't tell us what to do!* That is lecture 4's starting point ([from relativism to theories](#/libs7102-l4/from-relativism-to-theories)).
