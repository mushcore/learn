FDM gives every input signal its own band of the link's frequencies. Each input is shifted to a different carrier, the shifted signals are added, and the receiver filters them apart.

## FDM is analog and works by modulation

Slide wording: *An analog multiplexing technique. Analog signals generated by each sending device modulate different carrier frequencies. The modulated signals are combined into a single composite signal that can be transported by the link.*

Modulating a baseband signal onto a carrier moves its spectrum up to sit around that carrier (Week 5). Give each input a different carrier and their spectra land in different places, so adding them does not mix them. The sum is the composite signal.

The slide's video frame shows three message spectra $m_1(t)$, $m_2(t)$, $m_3(t)$ drawn as a triangle, a V and a half disc, each multiplied by its carrier $f_{c1}$, $f_{c2}$, $f_{c3}$ and summed. The output spectrum has the three shapes side by side at the three carrier frequencies.

## Each channel is a carrier frequency

The conceptual view draws the MUX feeding Channel 1, Channel 2 and Channel 3 as three coloured bands stacked on one link. Slide wording: *Each channel corresponds to a specific carrier frequency allocated to an individual signal source.*

## Guard bands keep channels apart

Slide wording: *Guard bands: Strips of unused bandwidth that separate channels from each other.* Their purpose: *To prevent signals from overlapping and interference.*

A guard band sits between two neighbouring channels, so $n$ channels need $n - 1$ guard bands. The outer edges of the first and last channel touch the ends of the link; nothing is guarded there.

## When FDM can be used

*FDM can be used when the bandwidth of a link (in hertz) is greater than the combined bandwidths of the signals to be transmitted.* The combined bandwidths include any guard bands, so the link must be at least $n \times B + (n - 1) \times G$ wide for $n$ channels of bandwidth $B$ with guard bands $G$.

## The FDM process and the demultiplexing process

| Stage | Sending end | Receiving end |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | each baseband analog signal enters a **modulator** with its own carrier $f_1$, $f_2$ or $f_3$ | a splitter copies the composite signal to each branch |
| 2 | the modulated (bandpass) signals meet at an adder, **+** | each branch's **filter** keeps only its channel's band |
| 3 | one composite signal travels on the link | each **demodulator**, with the same carrier $f_1$, $f_2$ or $f_3$, brings the signal back to baseband |

Modulator, add, link, filter, demodulate. The carrier used to shift a signal up is the carrier used to shift it back down.

## Applications

The slide lists three: telephone companies, AM and FM radio broadcasting, television broadcasting. Each radio or TV station is one channel on the shared air.

## Slide example: three voice channels on 20 to 32 kHz

*Assume that a voice channel occupies a bandwidth of 4 kHz. We need to combine three voice channels into a link with a bandwidth of 12 kHz, from 20 to 32 kHz. Show the configuration, using the frequency domain. Assume there are no guard bands.*

Three channels of 4 kHz need $3 \times 4 = 12$ kHz, exactly the link, so the bands sit edge to edge from 20 kHz up:

| Telephone | Baseband | Modulator shifts it to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 to 4 kHz | **20 to 24 kHz** |
| 2 | 0 to 4 kHz | **24 to 28 kHz** |
| 3 | 0 to 4 kHz | **28 to 32 kHz** |

The slide figure labels the sending box *Shift and combine*: the three shifted bands are added into one 20 to 32 kHz signal on the *Higher-bandwidth link*. The receiving box is *Filter and shift*: a bandpass filter for 20 to 24, one for 24 to 28 and one for 28 to 32 kHz, each followed by a shift back to 0 to 4 kHz for its telephone.

Channel $k$ starts at $20 + 4(k - 1)$ kHz, so channel 2 is 24 to 28 kHz. With guard bands each later channel would start higher by the guard band width.

## FDM without a physical multiplexer

Slide wording: *In many cases, no need for a physical multiplexer/demultiplexer.* Two examples:

- *In radio and TV broadcasting, stations agree to send their broadcasts to the air using different carrier frequencies.* The air is the link; the agreement does the multiplexing.
- *In case of cellular telephone systems, a base station assigns a carrier frequency to the telephone user (when the user hangs up, her/his bandwidth is assigned to another user).*

## Example 1: five channels with guard bands

*Five channels, each with a 100-kHz bandwidth, are to be multiplexed together. What is the minimum bandwidth of the link if there is a need for a guard band of 10 kHz between the channels to prevent interference?*

Five channels have four gaps between them, so *at least 4 guard bands*:

$$B_{link} = 5 \times 100 + 4 \times 10 = 500 + 40 = \mathbf{540\ kHz}$$

Counting five guard bands gives 550 kHz, the common wrong answer. Count the gaps, not the channels.
